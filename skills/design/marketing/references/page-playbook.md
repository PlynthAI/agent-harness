# Page playbook

How to turn an argument into a website or landing page: position first, then the
section order, then each section's job, then the look, then the checks. These are
defaults drawn from the sources at the end, restated in our own words. The
project's design authority and its own page patterns outrank every one of them.

## 1. Position before writing

A page cannot be clearer than its positioning. Settle these in order, each from
the one before, and never start from a slogan or a category:

1. **The alternative.** What the reader does today if this product did not
   exist, including doing nothing.
2. **What is different.** What this offers that the alternative lacks.
3. **What that is worth.** The outcome the difference produces for the reader.
4. **Who cares most.** The readers for whom that outcome matters enough to act.
5. **The frame.** The category or comparison in which that value is obvious.

The value proposition is a goal the reader already wants plus how the product
gets them there. The technology behind it is not a benefit: "powered by AI" says
nothing about the reader's goal. Collect objections before writing: ask
customers what almost stopped them, and ask people who did not buy why not.

Every section then does one of two things: raises the reader's desire, or lowers
the effort and confusion of acting. A section that does neither is cut.

## 2. Section order

The default order for a product page:

1. Navigation
2. Hero
3. Trust, directly under the hero
4. Product and features, led by problems
5. Proof: testimonials, results, case studies
6. Support as needed: how it works, comparison, pricing, questions
7. Final action
8. Footer

The sources differ in detail. One puts an action straight after the trust
block and repeats it later; another gives the hero two actions and places
testimonials after the features. Both agree on the principle: familiar order,
evidence next to each claim, and a clear action at the top and the bottom. A
long page is fine when the hero earns the scroll, but avoid a false floor that
makes the page look finished before it is.

Other registers change the order. A practice or portfolio site leads with the
work: selected projects, what the practice does and for whom, how an engagement
runs, proof through the projects themselves, and an enquiry path. An editorial
page leads with the argument and lets evidence carry the reader down. Name the
register first (the SKILL's "Decide how the page communicates"); this order is
the product-page default, not a template for every page.

## 3. Each section: job, evidence, failure

**Navigation.** Few items, labelled in the reader's words, the logo linking home,
one primary action. It fails with vague labels ("Solutions", "Resources") and
with two or three competing buttons.

**Hero.** Its job is to say what this is and why it matters within seconds. Its
budget: a heading, one supporting sentence, the primary action and at most one
lighter secondary action, the product or the image the reader came for, and at
most one small label. Build the heading from the contrast with the reader's
current alternative, as a specific claim or as the answer to the biggest
objection. The supporting sentence says how it works or why the claim is
believable. The action's label continues the promise ("Check a site", not "Get
started"). A centred hero is the common convention for product pages; a split
or full-bleed composition is a choice that needs a reason, and the project
authority decides. It fails as a slogan with no noun in it, as abstract art where
the product should be, or as three buttons.

**Trust.** Directly under the hero: customer or partner logos the owner may show,
or numbers with a source. Early on, one real testimonial beats a wall of
borrowed logos. Nothing here is ever invented.

**Product and features.** Three to six, each led by the problem it solves, not a
list of functions. Each has a plain heading that states the value, a short
paragraph that also answers an objection, and the product showing it working:
a real screen, a short loop, a real output. Each ties back to the hero's claim.
Vary the composition between features, and do not run the same layout family
more than twice in a row.

**Proof.** Curated testimonials with name, role and organisation; results with
their source; case studies where the work is the proof. Put proof next to the
claim it supports rather than in one distant block.

**Support.** How it works, a comparison with the alternative, pricing and
questions, only as the argument needs them. Pricing often lives on its own page;
on a landing page, plans sit side by side and a recommended plan says why.
Questions answer real objections (trial, data, security, access, cost of
switching), usually as an accordion near the end.

**Final action.** One distinct, full-width block that repeats the primary action
with the same intent as the hero's. Where a conversation sells better than a
sign-up, booking a call is the action.

**Footer.** A second chance and a last resort: contact and legal links, a repeat
of the main navigation, secondary tasks, social links and a newsletter where the
project has one. No more than two levels, no vague labels, and legible contrast.

## 4. Look and feel

Left alone, a model produces the average of the web: the safe, familiar page
that could belong to anyone. Counter it by committing to the project's
direction, not by adding effects:

- **Type:** the project's faces, with a decisive scale rather than timid steps.
- **Colour:** the project's tokens, a dominant ground and one sharp accent, not
  an even spread.
- **Motion:** one considered entrance rather than scattered effects; content is
  visible without it, and reduced motion is honoured.
- **Depth:** backgrounds and imagery with atmosphere where the brand allows it;
  a quiet brand stays quiet.

Set the direction through the design router's staged workflow. An explicitly
requested Impeccable command keeps its own direction and review contract.
Gather relevant references per section from the
galleries in the design library's sources catalogue, and write down which
decisions are taken from each - spacing, type, pacing - rather than copying a
motif. References stay internal; a screenshot or another company's copy is
never shipped.

## 5. Page checks

Run these before the rendered-page checks in the SKILL's "Verify in the real
page".

- **Clarity:** after the hero, can someone outside the market say what is sold
  and to whom? Can someone inside it say how it differs?
- **Actions:** one label per action intent across navigation, hero, final action
  and footer; labels are specific ("Learn more" and "Click here" are not) and fit
  on one line at desktop width.
- **Facts:** every number, customer name, logo and testimonial traces to a
  source. Sample content is marked as sample in drafts and never ships.
- **Rhythm:** no more than one small label per three sections, no layout family
  more than twice in a row, one accent on the page.
- **Delivery:** content visible before any animation; images sized so nothing
  shifts, and only the first screen's images loaded eagerly; visible focus,
  adequate hit targets and an ordered heading outline; sentence case for
  marketing headings unless the project says otherwise.
