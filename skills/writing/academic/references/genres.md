# Genres and structure

Pick the genre at intake. The venue's own article types and the university's
thesis regulations win over these spines.

## Genre spines

| Genre | Spine | The contribution | Reviewers catch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Position paper | Stance and stakes; the debate; the argument in steps, each supported; counter-positions answered; implications | A defensible stance that changes how the field acts or thinks | Assertion without support; a straw-man opponent |
| Theory or conceptual paper | The problem with existing concepts; lineage; the framework (definitions, relations, a diagram); illustration on cases; its limits; implications | A new concept, framework or reframing | A framework never tested against a case; terms that drift |
| Design-research paper | Question and context; approach and its justification; the design work documented; what was learned and from what evidence; reflection and transferability | Knowledge produced by or about designing, not the artefact alone | A project report with no research question; one case generalised |
| Empirical study | Introduction (gap, question); methods (setting, participants or data, procedure, analysis, ethics); results in question order; discussion (meaning, comparison, limitations, implications) | Findings | Methods too thin to repeat; interpretation inside results |
| Review | See [literature](literature.md) | Synthesis and agenda | Summary without synthesis |

### Design research

Frayling's distinction names the stance: research **into** design studies
design as a subject, research **through** design produces knowledge by
designing, and research **for** design informs a design. Say which, because
reviewers judge the evidence differently for each.

| Approach | Evidence it rests on | State in the method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Research through design | Design iterations, decisions, critiques and reflection | How the designing was documented and how reflection was structured |
| Practice-based | A body of work with an exegesis | What the work contributes that the text alone could not |
| Case study | One or several bounded cases, multiple data sources | Case selection, boundaries and why the case is informative |
| Action research | Cycles of plan, act, observe, reflect with participants | Who took part, their role, consent, and what changed each cycle |
| Participatory or co-design | Community or stakeholder sessions and their outputs | Recruitment, power and voice, consent, how outputs were analysed |

Name the designer-researcher's dual role and how its bias was handled.

### Empirical studies

IMRaD here is lighter than in biomedical work. Use a reporting guideline only
when the venue requires one (for example SRQR or COREQ for qualitative work)
and then open its current official checklist.

## Journal or conference

| | Journal | Conference paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contribution | Complete, positioned against the field | One focused contribution, often early or in progress |
| Length | The guide's word limit | A hard page limit in the venue template |
| Review | Rounds of revision with a response letter | One round, sometimes a short rebuttal |
| Examples | Design Studies, The Journal of Architecture, Automation in Construction, Frontiers of Architectural Research, Architectural Science Review, CoDesign, Buildings | CAADRIA, eCAADe, ACADIA, Design Computing and Cognition (DCC) |

Fit the paper to the venue's aims and scope as published, not to its
reputation. They differ in register: a computational or construction journal
expects validation and data, a design or theory journal expects argument and
positioning. Read recent issues or proceedings of the target venue before
drafting.

## PhD thesis

| Monograph | Thesis by publication |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Introduction: problem, questions, contribution, chapter map | 1. Introduction or exegesis: problem, questions, how the papers answer them |
| 2. Literature and context | 2. Context and methodology across the papers |
| 3. Methodology: stance, approach, methods, ethics | 3 to n. Papers as chapters, each opened by a linking preface and a statement of each author's contribution |
| 4 to n. Findings or design-work chapters | n+1. Integrative discussion: what the papers establish together |
| n+1. Discussion | n+2. Conclusion: contributions, limitations, further work |
| n+2. Conclusion: contributions, limitations, further work | |

Each chapter opens with its role in the argument and closes by handing to the
next. A practice-based thesis pairs the body of work with an exegesis. Check the
university's rules on word limits, by-publication eligibility and including
published versions under the publisher's copyright.

## Title, abstract and keywords

- The title names the subject and the contribution.
- Write the abstract last: context, gap, aim, approach, main finding or argument,
  contribution. Use the venue's structured headings when it has them. Every claim
  in the abstract appears in the body.
- Choose keywords a searcher would use, not title words; the Getty Art &
  Architecture Thesaurus helps with architectural terms.

## Argument

- State the contribution on the first page and repeat it in the same terms in
  the discussion and conclusion.
- Open each section with what it establishes.
- Match verbs to the evidence outline: "shows" for supported claims, "suggests"
  or "indicates" for partial ones, "we propose" for speculative ones.
- Name limitations concretely: one case, one region, self-reported data, the
  designer as researcher, a tool version that has since changed.

## Figures as evidence

Drawings, photographs, BIM screenshots, renders and diagrams are evidence.

- Record each figure's creator, date, source, software and version for model
  outputs, and permission or licence for anything not the authors' own.
- Refer to every figure in the text and say what it shows.
- Label renders and speculative images as proposals, never as built outcomes.
- Keep figures legible at print size and readable without colour alone; add alt
  text where the venue supports it.

## Cross-section audit

| Check | Passes when |
| --- | --- |
| Abstract and conclusions | Every abstract claim is argued in the body and appears in the conclusions |
| Questions and answers | Each question in the introduction is answered or explicitly left open |
| Methods and results | Every method reported has results, and every result has a method |
| Figures and tables | Each is numbered in order, referenced in the text, and matches the prose |
| Citations | Every in-text citation is in the reference list and the reverse ([citations](../../citations/METHOD.md)) |
| Terms | Each key term is defined once and used the same way throughout |
| Limitations | They bound the claims actually made |
